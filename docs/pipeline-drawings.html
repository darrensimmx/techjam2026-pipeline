How the agent actually works

Six plates covering the whole repo: what ships, what drives it, what happens inside a turn, where the score comes from, and what is still unbuilt. Every number here was measured against data/public_set.jsonl (200 sessions) this session.

PLATE 1

One class crosses the line

Almost everything in the repo is scaffolding. The submission is a single Python class, starter/agent.py::Agent, which the organizer imports and calls in-process — no network, no CLI, no server. Knowing which side of this line a file sits on tells you whether a bug in it can cost you points.

PLATE 2

The scored loop

A session is at most ten turns of one conversation with a simulated customer. That customer is code — customer_reply() in the vendored evaluator — holding a hidden card of preferences it discloses only when asked. The session ends the instant the target product appears in your top-10.

PLATE 3

Inside one turn

Two independent lanes run every turn and meet at the validator. The retrieval lane turns the whole conversation so far into one search query; the ask lane picks the next question off a fixed list. Neither uses a model, and nothing touches the network.

PLATE 4

Why four of the six questions are dead

The scheduler asks a fixed order: material → feature → color → style → size → use_case. Whether an ask yields anything depends on how classify_constraint() happens to bucket the four strings on the customer's hidden card — and that distribution is extremely lopsided.

category and brand are outside classify_constraint's range entirely — asking them can never yield, in any session. budget is reachable in principle but no target in the public set carries a price.

The "other" wildcard, and why it is declined

Asking ask_attribute: "other" switches the bucket filter off — the evaluator's condition is attribute == "other" or classify_constraint(value) == attribute — so it returns the next two undisclosed constraints whatever they are. Replayed over all 200 sessions, an all-"other" policy drains the hidden card in 200/200 sessions versus 134/200 for the fixed six. That is the +0.042 recorded in docs/hard-rules.md, and it is rejected as harness-gaming: it exploits one line of the simulator rather than asking a question a real customer could answer.

PLATE 5

Where the score comes from

TechnicalScore is 0.50 × HitRate@10 + 0.30 × MRR + 0.20 × efficiency. Hit rate saturates early — by turn 5 the top-10 has everything it will ever have — while the target sits inside the top-100 far more often than inside the top-10. That gap is the whole opportunity.

Oracle arms are ceilings — a perfect reranker over that depth, not something you can build. The cross-encoder is the one arm actually measured, at roughly 1.2 s per turn; shipping it is blocked on an unpublished organizer timeout. Anything that reorders results without changing what is retrieved competes in this column.

Do not quote 0.693 without this caveat

public_set.jsonl carries no real intent_card, so the evaluator builds the simulated customer's "hidden" preferences out of the target product's own listing and recites them back to you turn by turn. 94% of disclosed constraint strings are exact substrings of the target's indexed text. A frozen agent that reads only turn 1 already scores 0.185 against the organizer's published 0.125 baseline. The local number is an upper bracket, not a score — scripts/leak_controlled_benchmark.py brackets it honestly.

Phase 3 is paused because its premise is contested: only 40 of 200 sessions reach turn 7 at all, so "the schedule runs dry after turn 6" describes a problem most sessions never have.

The seven hard rules — three are unimplemented

RuleWhat it bindsState
1Never send ask_attribute: nullviolated — 160 null turns
2Always return the full top-10honored
3Parse replies with regex, never a modelhonored
4Split refusal from exhaustion on additionalnot built
5Always re-ask a burned attributenot built
6Accumulate verbatim, never erase on overridehonored
7Do not build a scenario classifierhonored

Rules 1, 4 and 5 are one connected piece of work in two files, and they are the cheapest points on the board. Two events burn an ask without ever reading it — a boundary refusal (10 sessions) and the override turn (30 sessions, where evaluate() never calls customer_reply at all) — and the [:2] disclosure cap strands a third constraint in 59 sessions. Together those account for almost exactly the 66 sessions whose hidden card is never fully drained.

The order I would work in

  1. Deep rerank — retrieve 100, rerank to 10. The only lever with a large measured number behind it.
  2. Rules 1, 4, 5 — deterministic, no new dependency, recovers constraints in 25 of the 40 burned sessions.
  3. Attribute weights as ask-order priors — computable offline from the catalog, slotting behind the existing next_attribute(state) signature the scheduler was designed to accept.
  4. Any model, strictly off the critical path — its own try/except with a regex fallback, per the rule in CLAUDE.md.
